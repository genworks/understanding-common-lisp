\chapter{Where to Go Next}

This book has covered the ground you need in order to start. Here is
where to go for the rest.

\section{Reference}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The Common Lisp HyperSpec}\index{HyperSpec},
\url{https://www.lispworks.com/documentation/HyperSpec/Front/index.htm}. The
text of the standard as a hypertext document. It is the final word on
what any standard function does. It is written for implementers, and
its entries are worth learning to read. Your editor can open the
entry for the name at the cursor.

\item \textbf{The Common Lisp Cookbook}\index{Cookbook}, at\\
\url{https://lispcookbook.github.io/cl-cookbook/}. Task-oriented
chapters on strings, files, dates, regular expressions, testing, web
development, and much else, kept up to date by the community. This is
the best next stop after this book.

\item \textbf{The manual for your implementation.} Each one documents
its extensions, its compiler, its debugger and its profiler. SBCL's
is at \url{https://www.sbcl.org/manual/}.

\item \textbf{The ASDF manual}, \url{https://asdf.common-lisp.dev},
for everything about defining and building systems.
\end{itemize}

\section{Books}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Practical Common Lisp}, Peter Seibel, Apress, 2005. A
full introduction for working programmers, with chapters that build
real programs. Free to read at \url{https://gigamonkeys.com/book/}.

\item \textbf{Common Lisp: A Gentle Introduction to Symbolic
Computation}, David S. Touretzky. Starts from nothing and goes
slowly. A good choice if this book moved too fast. Free at
\url{https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~dst/LispBook/}.

\item \textbf{ANSI Common Lisp}, Paul Graham, Prentice Hall, 1996. A
compact tutorial followed by a compact reference.

\item \textbf{On Lisp}, Paul Graham, Prentice Hall, 1993. The classic
book about macros and building languages on Lisp. Free at
\url{https://www.paulgraham.com/onlisp.html}.

\item \textbf{Paradigms of Artificial Intelligence Programming},
Peter Norvig, Morgan Kaufmann, 1992. Teaches programming at a high
level through substantial programs in Common Lisp. Free at
\url{https://github.com/norvig/paip-lisp}.

\item \textbf{Object-Oriented Programming in Common Lisp}, Sonya
Keene, Addison-Wesley, 1989. The standard introduction to CLOS.

\item \textbf{Common Lisp Recipes}, Edmund Weitz, Apress, 2016.
Hundreds of specific problems and their solutions, with explanations
of why.
\end{itemize}

\section{Libraries and Examples}

To find a library for a job, start with the Awesome CL list,
\url{https://github.com/CodyReichert/awesome-cl}, which sorts the
well-regarded libraries by subject, or search Quicklisp from the REPL
with \code{ql:system-apropos}. CLiki, \url{https://www.cliki.net}, is
the community's long-running wiki.

The best way to get better at Lisp is to read programs. Every library
you load with Quicklisp is on your disk in source form, and
\code{M-.} in your editor takes you from any function's name to its
definition. Read the code of the libraries you use. Much of it is
short and very good.

For complete applications to read and run, see the sites above, and
for engineering and web applications built with Gendl, the examples at
\url{https://github.com/genworks/demos}.

\section{Community}

\url{https://common-lisp.net} hosts many of the central projects and
has pointers to the rest. Planet Lisp, \url{https://planet.lisp.org},
collects the blogs of people who write about Lisp. Questions are
welcome in the \code{r/Common\_Lisp} forum on Reddit, on the Lisp
Discord server, and in the \code{\#commonlisp} channel on the
Libera.Chat IRC network. The European Lisp Symposium meets every
spring, and its papers and talks are online.

\section{A Last Word}

The way of working this book has described, with a running image, a
REPL, and a program built and changed a piece at a time, will feel odd
for a few days if you are used to compiling and running whole
programs. Stay with it. Start small, try each piece as you write it,
ask the running Lisp when you are not sure, and let the language grow
toward your problem.
